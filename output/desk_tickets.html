Reflection

Q1. How well did the agents perform?

Overall, the agents were better at diagnosing problems than at completing operational workflows.

  • Ticket 101: They found the size-S tee shortage and the $840 invoice 501, and the invoice was paid after my approval. But resolving the ticket took 8 runs, and the customer response, restock order, receipt, and fulfilment were simulated manager events, not real ones.
  • Ticket 102: They handled the $2,400 rent payment well: one team run, my approval, and one Accounting payment run.
  • Ticket 103: They found the 12-hoodie shortage (8 of 20 size M on hand) and proposed the 10% discount ($52.20, above the $22 cost) that I approved. But after I rejected the $264 restock (BUY-9688DD9C), Inventory filed the same request again (BUY-FF090480) in the next run. I approved it and then cancelled it, because $264 was more than the $160 left in checking.

This showed me that good reasoning isn't enough. Agents must respect human decisions, avoid repeating rejected actions, and understand what "done" actually means.

Q2. Expected vs. Actual

  • Ticket 101: Inventory was consulted first, as I expected, and Boss → Customer Service happened too. But the Inventory → Accounting delegation I predicted never occurred. Instead, the Boss went to Accounting directly, and Customer Service also consulted Inventory and Accounting. The process was more complicated than planned because what "Resolved" meant was unclear at first, so it took 8 runs.
  • Ticket 102: Actual closely matched Expected. Facilities went first, and both Boss → Facilities and Facilities → Accounting happened (the Boss also checked with Accounting directly). The agents verified the rent, Accounting requested the payment, and it was paid after my approval. My key point held: after rent and invoice 501, checking ended at $160.
  • Ticket 103: Inventory was consulted first, not Customer Service as I predicted. Boss → Customer Service and Customer Service → Inventory happened as expected, but the Boss consulted Accounting directly instead of through Customer Service. The agents also re-filed the restock request after my rejection.

My main lesson: workflow design has to plan for exceptions, rejected decisions, and requests that can't be fully fulfilled, not just the ideal delegation sequence.

Q3. Single agent vs. multi-agent

Ticket 102 would have been simpler with a single Accounting-focused agent. The job was to verify the $2,400 rent, check the $3,400 balance, get my approval, and pay, leaving $1,000. Instead, the Boss, Facilities, and Accounting took two runs and about 46,700 tokens.

The extra agents added coordination overhead without improving the decision. I would use one agent for straightforward transactions and multiple specialists when inventory, supplier, financial, and customer constraints interact, as in Tickets 101 and 103. Agent architecture should match decision complexity, not the number of business departments.

Q4. Three new problems the current team could solve

  • A student requests 15 Crest Mugs, which currently have 0 on hand. Inventory could identify the shortage, check vendors, and file a restock for my approval, building on Ticket 101.
  • A supplier blocks a new order because of an unpaid invoice. Inventory and Accounting could investigate the restriction and arrange an approved payment, like invoice 501 in Ticket 101.
  • Another student club asks for 30 discounted hoodies. Customer Service could propose pricing, Inventory could check stock, and Accounting could check affordability. As Ticket 103 showed, the team must respect a rejected purchase instead of requesting it again.

These problems show the value of coordinating several operational constraints with the tools we already built.

Q5. Three new problems the current team could not solve

  • Demand forecasting. Inventory can see current shortages but can't predict future demand, and the database has no sales history. A Forecasting Agent with historical sales and seasonal analysis tools would be needed.
  • Flexible return policies. Suppose Campus Customs allows returns within 14 days, and a student asks to return a defective product after 16 days. The system has no tools to check purchase history, manage returns, or evaluate policy exceptions. I would add a Returns Agent and return-management tools, with human approval required for exceptions.
  • End-to-end fulfilment. Ticket 101 relied on simulated receipt and fulfilment, and Ticket 103 ended with none of the 20 hoodies delivered. A Fulfilment Agent with real shipping, delivery tracking, and approved customer communication tools would be needed.

One important lesson from Problem 9 was that our first definition of "Resolved" was unclear, which led to unnecessary runs and simulated actions. I would track "Decision Complete" and "Fulfilment Complete" separately, so finishing an internal decision isn't confused with actually satisfying the customer.
